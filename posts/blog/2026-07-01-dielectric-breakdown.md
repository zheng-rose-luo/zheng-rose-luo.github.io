# Making Electrical Defect Tolerance Measurable

Electrical breakdown measurements depend on material properties, specimen geometry and the defects present in the test. My doctoral work asks whether an energetic description can help connect these factors and predict failure under controlled conditions.

The theoretical calculation gives a driving force for the advance of an idealised conducting channel. The experimental challenge is to establish what resistance to that advance can be inferred from measurements, and how far that inference transfers to a different test.

## Fabrication is part of the scientific question

My initial approach used fine needles inserted manually into thin specimens. I led a small undergraduate team in these trials, but the method did not provide the geometric precision needed for the comparisons I wanted to make.

I subsequently examined alternative fabrication routes, contacted manufacturers and developed collaborations involving femtosecond laser processing, electrical engineering and atomic layer deposition. The aim was to create controlled specimens in batches and study variability systematically.

This changed how I understood the problem. A theoretical prediction becomes testable only when the relevant geometry can be made, characterised and reproduced sufficiently well.

## What a transferable resistance would require

A value inferred from one test can describe that test without necessarily predicting another. A stronger assessment uses a resistance inferred under one condition to predict measurements under different controlled geometries or loading conditions.

That comparison should account for geometric uncertainty, material variability and the distinction between a prescribed artificial defect and uncontrolled flaws. Cross-condition consistency remains an active question in this work.

## Comparing material designs

A connected study compares material formulations in straight-edge and conducting-tip chip configurations. Under the conditions examined, the formulation-dependent voltage change was larger in the tip-containing configuration. This motivates further tests of how material structure affects the response to an introduced defect.

Mapping those voltages into an energy-based quantity helps express the results in the model's terms. Because the mapping uses the same voltage measurements, it does not provide an additional independent experimental test. Further controls are needed to connect the observed response to a microscopic mechanism.
