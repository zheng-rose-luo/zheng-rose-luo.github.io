# Distilling Bayesian Priors into Neural Networks

A reading note on [McCoy and Griffiths (2025), *Modeling rapid language learning by distilling Bayesian priors into artificial neural networks*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-59957-y). The observations below describe their model and evaluations; the personal connection at the end is a question prompted by the paper.

## What the Six Figures Tell Us

**Figure 1** is the roadmap. In the modelling setting considered here, the authors seek to combine strong inductive biases with flexible representations. They sample artificial languages from a Bayesian prior and use them as meta-learning tasks for a neural network.

**Figure 2** gets into the mechanics. It uses MAML, a meta-learning algorithm whose core idea is to teach the model *how to learn new things*. The model is exposed to many different artificial languages, each time with only a few examples, and asked to adapt quickly. The end product is a good set of initial parameters — with this starting point, a few gradient steps on a new language are enough to get good performance.

**Figure 3** shows the first evaluation. Across 56 formal languages, the prior-trained network approaches the Bayesian learner's data efficiency; the standard network needs roughly ten times as many examples for comparable performance. The reported timing comparison concerns learning an individual formal language, rather than a universal inference speedup. It does not include the cost of creating the meta-trained initialization.

**Figure 4** switches to an 8.6-million-word corpus of child-directed English. The prior-trained network outperforms the 5-gram baseline, with a small improvement over the standard network on the full corpus. The benefit depends jointly on model size and data quantity; it does not simply increase whenever data are reduced.

**Figure 5** examines targeted linguistic evaluations, including deeper recursion and priming. The prior-trained network performs better on most of the tested recursion conditions and shows stronger priming: a repeated sentence becomes less surprising to the model. Stronger priming is a behavioural difference to explain, rather than a general measure of superior language ability.

**Figure 6** changes the primitives used to generate meta-training tasks. Removing recursion or synchrony impairs relevant formal-language evaluations, supporting an effect of the targeted biases. The priming result is more complicated: removing synchrony does not remove the enhanced priming, so that proposed explanation remains unresolved.

The sequence is useful to me: **define a modelling problem, construct a candidate solution, evaluate it in different settings, and use interventions to examine particular explanations**. The final step can reveal where an explanation is incomplete.

## What This Paper Does

The authors use a structured distribution of learning tasks to shape a network's inductive biases. For me, the interesting question is how a training history can change subsequent generalisation, and how interventions on that history help test an explanation of the change.

## My Reflection

A short video changed my mind. I had assumed that vertical walls did not get dirty, until I watched someone power-washing a park wall. I noticed my expectation changing immediately. That experience makes me curious about how prior knowledge supports rapid revision after an unexpected observation.

The connection to this paper is a research question, not an identification of the same mechanism. The authors explicitly use meta-learning as a tool for constructing a model with chosen biases; they do not claim that human linguistic biases must be acquired through MAML. I would like to understand what behavioural experiment could distinguish different explanations of rapid belief revision.

# 读McCoy & Griffiths (2025)：当贝叶斯先验被蒸馏进神经网络

这是一篇读书笔记。论文中的模型结果与文末的个人联想需要分开理解。[原文链接](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-59957-y)。

## 六张图在说什么

**图1**是整篇论文的“地图”。在这项工作的建模设置中，作者希望结合较强的归纳偏置与灵活的表征：从贝叶斯先验中采样人工语言，再把它们作为神经网络的元学习任务。

**图2**讲具体怎么操作。用的是MAML这个元学习算法，核心思路就是让模型学会"怎么学新东西"。模型被放到很多不同的人工语言上训练，每次只给少量例句，然后要求它快速适应。最终产出一个好的初始参数——有了这个起点，遇到新语言时随便更新几步就能上手。

**图3**比较56种形式语言上的学习表现。先验训练网络的数据效率接近贝叶斯学习器，普通网络达到相近表现需要约十倍的例子。文中的时间比较指学习单个形式语言的时间，不是普遍的推理提速，也不包含产生元学习初始化的成本。

**图4**使用860万词的儿童导向英语语料。先验训练网络优于5-gram基线，在完整语料上相对普通网络的提升较小。收益同时取决于模型规模和数据量，不能概括成“数据越少，优势一定越大”。

**图5**进行针对性的语言评价，包括更深的递归和启动效应。先验训练网络在多数被测递归条件上表现更好，并显示更强的启动效应：重复出现的句子对模型而言更不意外。更强的启动效应是一种需要解释的行为差异，不能直接当作普遍更好的语言能力。

**图6**改变生成元训练任务的基本规则。移除递归或同步规则会损害相关形式语言上的表现，支持目标偏置产生了作用。但移除同步规则并没有消除增强的启动效应，对这一现象的原有解释仍不充分。

我关注的是这套研究顺序：**界定建模问题、构建候选模型、在不同场景下评价表现，再通过干预检验具体解释**。最后一步也可能揭示解释还缺少什么。

## 整篇论文在做什么

作者通过有结构的学习任务分布塑造网络的归纳偏置。我更关心由此提出的问题：一段训练历史如何改变之后的泛化，以及改变这段历史如何帮助检验对这种变化的解释？

## 我的感受

我曾经以为垂直的墙面不会脏，直到看到高压水枪清洗公园墙壁的视频。我能察觉自己的预期当场发生了变化。这让我好奇：已有知识如何帮助人在遇到意外观察时迅速修正判断？

这段体验与论文的联系仍是一个问题，不能据此认定它们具有相同机制。作者明确把元学习用作构建具有特定偏置的模型的工具，并没有断言人类语言偏置必须通过MAML获得。我更想进一步思考：什么样的行为实验能够区分对快速信念修正的不同解释？
